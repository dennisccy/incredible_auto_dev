## 34. A port (or cwd, or command line) treated as proof that a process is yours to kill

**Pattern:** every framework service teardown decided what to terminate from the *address* of the thing it wanted gone, never from evidence that this run had started it. `kill_phase_servers` ran `fuser -k -9 $CHAIN_BACKEND_PORT/tcp`; `cleanup_dev_servers` added three `pkill -f` command-line patterns; `reclaim_canonical_phase_ports` freed the canonical offset pair at engine start; `kill_stale_backend_server` matched `/proc/<pid>/cwd` against the checkout. Each looked narrow — "only this project's ports", "only this project's directory" — and each was wrong in the same way. Ports are derived from `sha1(project_root)`, so they are a property of the **project**, not of the **run**; the operator's own backend and frontend, started by `scripts/dev.sh` or a product-side pump, bind exactly those ports. On 2026-09-15 a trading_workstation goal session killed the product's live backend and frontend on :8319/:3319 repeatedly across one session — 35 dev-phase sweeps, both services answering `status: 000` three minutes later — and then restarted them as if it had started them.

**Why it fails:** an address is a rendezvous point, not an identity. Anything can be at it: your service, a previous run's orphan, the operator's stack, an unrelated app. The narrowing that makes these kills feel safe ("same port", "same directory", "same command name") narrows the *candidate set* and says nothing about *provenance* — and provenance is the only thing that licenses a kill. The failure is invisible in the framework's own logs, because `fuser -k` prints nothing and the very next step boots a replacement, so a destroyed product service looks exactly like a cold start. Worse, the blind sweep and the port-drift logic propped each other up: the free-port scan drifted away from a busy canonical port, which made forcibly freeing that port look necessary, which made the sweep look load-bearing.

**Prevention:** applies to any cleanup that terminates a process it did not spawn in the same shell.
- Kill by **provenance**, never by address. Stamp the environment of everything you launch (`CHAIN_SERVICE_OWNER_SCOPE`, `CHAIN_SERVICE_INSTANCE`) and read `/proc/<pid>/environ` at teardown. environ is inherited across fork *and* exec, so the stamp reaches wrapper-launched children, descendants reparented to init, and servers an **agent** started inside your dispatch — while a process that merely recycled a recorded pid has no stamp at all. This is also why a stale record cannot authorize a kill: the record is not the authority, the live process's environment is.
- A registry record is **observability, not permission**. If permission comes from a file, registry health becomes a safety dependency and you must choose between unsafe kills and a wedged pipeline. Derive authority from a direct procfs read and a corrupt registry can do neither.
- "No record" is **not** "free to kill". Anything started outside your framework is permanently recordless, so a legacy-compatibility escape hatch of that shape is not a migration state — it is a standing licence against exactly the processes you most need to protect.
- Be all-or-nothing per address: if you cannot account for **every** listener on a port, do not kill the ones you recognise and hope.
- When you may not reclaim an address, **say so** — name the pid, the command and the remedies. Do not silently relocate to a neighbouring port: that quietly tests a different service and breaks paired services whose addresses were configured at each other's startup.
- Ask what the sweep was really compensating for. Here it was "an agent leaked a verification server". Fix the launch contract (agents stop what they started, by pid) and let the ownership stamp reap the rest — do not compensate with collateral.

**Example (bad):** `fuser -k -9 "$CHAIN_BACKEND_PORT/tcp"` · `pkill -f "next dev -p $PORT"` · `cwd == "$be_dir" && kill -9 "$pid"` — three spellings of "it is at the address I wanted, so it is mine".
**Example (good):** `service_owner_terminate "$port" "<caller>"` — enumerate the listeners, require `/proc/<pid>/environ` to carry this lifecycle's stamp for every one of them, then kill by pid tree; refuse the whole port otherwise and report a named blocker.

**Detection:** a service you did not restart is suddenly down right after a pipeline step, and the engine log shows `not healthy (status: 000)` for a service that was healthy moments earlier; `grep -rn 'fuser -k\|pkill \|killall ' scripts/` returns anything outside a test; a teardown helper takes a port or a directory as its only argument. Regression test: `tests/automation/test-service-ownership.sh` B2 (an unowned listener survives every teardown path) and B12 (static sweep for reintroduced port/pattern kills).
